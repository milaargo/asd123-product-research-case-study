# ASD123 Product Research

Product research for an AI-supported speech-development app, from constrained-access research strategy to shipped product recommendations.

## Project Snapshot

**Role:** Research Lead  
**Project:** ASD123  
**Research sprint:** Oct–Nov 2023  
**Product launch:** March 2026  
**Impact follow-up:** August 2026  

**Methods:** Competitive analysis, stakeholder interviews, qualitative synthesis, affinity mapping, research-to-design translation

**Participants:** 7
- 4 speech-language pathologists
- 2 guardians
- 1 special-education teacher

## The Problem

ASD123 began as an AI-supported speech-development product built around voice-powered activities for clinical and at-home use.

As the product evolved, the founders wanted to make the experience more intuitive for children and guardians using it outside a clinical setting while preserving the structure that made it useful to speech-language pathologists.

The research question became:

> How might ASD123 support independent and at-home use without losing the therapeutic structure of the clinician experience?

## My Role

I led the research strategy during a three-week product sprint.

My responsibilities included:

- reframing the research plan when direct access to existing child users was restricted;
- independently recruiting relevant research participants;
- conducting stakeholder interviews;
- benchmarking comparable products;
- synthesizing findings across stakeholder groups;
- identifying six product opportunities;
- translating research into recommendations for the UI team.

## The Constraint Changed the Research Strategy

Direct access to ASD123's existing child users was restricted because the product served children with speech-development and special needs.

The founders also joined the sprint five days after work began, leaving a little over one week to recruit participants, conduct research, synthesize the evidence, and hand recommendations to the design team.

Rather than treat the lack of child-user access as a reason to abandon primary research, I reframed the study around the ecosystem surrounding the child.

I recruited:

- speech-language pathologists for therapeutic structure and clinical context;
- guardians for home routines, interests, sensory needs, and daily friction;
- a special-education teacher for learning and engagement patterns.

I then triangulated those perspectives with competitive and market research.

## What the Evidence Changed

Three broader insights emerged.

### 1. Motivation had to be immediate

Long-term therapeutic progress was often too abstract to motivate children in the moment. Visible progress, praise, rewards, and short-term accomplishment helped create buy-in.

### 2. Engagement was highly individual

Interests, sensory preferences, attention, and tolerance for distraction varied substantially from child to child. Choice and flexibility therefore needed to be part of the experience rather than optional additions.

### 3. The product had to carry more of the support

Outside therapy, structure, pacing, predictability, and progress cues could no longer depend on a clinician being present. More of that guidance needed to live inside the product.

Together, the findings pointed toward **guided flexibility**: enough structure to orient and motivate children, with enough adaptability to meet different needs.

## From Research to Product Decisions

The research produced six product opportunities:

1. Activity roadmap and visible progression
2. Onboarding assessment
3. Broader activity choice
4. Rewards and milestones
5. Audio guidance
6. Accessibility and parent controls

Two research-backed recommendations were ultimately implemented in the launched product:

- **Activity roadmap**

## Result

The redesigned product launched in March 2026.

During a later founder follow-up, ASD123 reported positive qualitative feedback around the roadmap and child engagement. The founder described the research as helping bridge the clinician experience with the parent-and-child experience.

## What I Would Change Now

The project also exposed gaps in the original research process.

If I were running it today, I would:

- define success metrics before design handoff;
- test low-fidelity concepts earlier;
- incorporate observed behavior alongside interviews;
- build continuous usability testing into the product cycle;
- combine observational research with surveys and product analytics.
