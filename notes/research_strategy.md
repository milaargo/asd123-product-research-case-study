# Research Strategy

## The Original Plan

The project initially appeared to call for direct user research with children and families using ASD123 at home.

That approach changed when access to ASD123's existing child user base was restricted.

Because the product served children with speech-development and special needs, I did not want to substitute convenience participants who lacked meaningful context.

## The Research Pivot

Instead, I reframed the study around the system surrounding the child.

Different participant groups could answer different parts of the problem:

- **Guardians** could describe routines, interests, sensory preferences, distractions, and what practicing at home actually looked like.
- **Speech-language pathologists** could explain therapeutic structure, repetition, progress, and how individual children's needs varied.
- **A special-education teacher** could provide evidence about motivation, predictability, reinforcement, and engagement in learning environments.

I recruited and interviewed 7 participants:

- 4 speech-language pathologists
- 2 guardians
- 1 special-education teacher

The sample was small, so I did not treat isolated observations as broad user truths. I looked for recurring patterns and convergence across participant groups.

## Why Competitive Research Was Necessary

The stakeholder interviews explained the environment surrounding the child, but they could not show how ASD123 compared with existing products.

I therefore benchmarked comparable speech-development and children's learning products.

The analysis served two purposes:

1. identify conventions users might already expect;
2. understand where ASD123 had a meaningful differentiator.

ASD123's clearest differentiator was its use of voice-command activities.

The competitive landscape also highlighted gaps around progress tracking, rewards, parent support, onboarding, and guided activity flow.

## Why I Combined the Evidence

No individual method was sufficient.

Competitive research showed the market.

Guardians described home life.

Clinicians described therapeutic structure.

The educator described engagement in a learning environment.

I combined those evidence sources through qualitative synthesis and affinity mapping, not to create themes for their own sake, but to identify recurring conditions that should influence the product direction.

The synthesis ultimately surfaced six recurring factors:

- distraction;
- sensory flexibility;
- variety and choice;
- visibility of progress;
- predictability;
- immediate motivation.

Those became the bridge between research evidence and the product recommendations.
