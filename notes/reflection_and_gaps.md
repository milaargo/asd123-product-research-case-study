# Reflection, Tradeoffs, and Current Research Plan

## The Biggest Limitation

The most important limitation was the lack of direct behavioral evidence from ASD123's existing child users during the research sprint.

The stakeholder-ecosystem strategy gave me useful evidence.

It did not replace direct observation.

If I were running the project in a commercial environment today, I would be much more explicit with the founders about that limitation.

I would say:

> If the research team cannot directly observe or interview current users, we need another route for testing behavior before substantial design effort is invested.

That might mean giving a clinician or founder a low-fidelity prototype to test with an appropriate participant and returning structured observations to the team.

Even one narrowly scoped concept test could reduce risk before moving into high fidelity.

## Where the Bootcamp Context Shaped the Work

The project moved quickly into a polished prototype.

That made sense in the context of a bootcamp, where the team needed complete artifacts and the UI designers wanted to demonstrate their work.

In a real product environment, I would question that sequence.

High-fidelity design is expensive.

I would rather test the underlying product hypothesis earlier.

The later product history reinforces this.

The roadmap concept survived, but the final live version was visually different.

That suggests the **research direction** was valuable even though the original interface was not something the business needed to preserve exactly.

## Breadth Versus Depth

I originally wanted to prioritize fewer recommendations and develop them more deeply.

The UI team felt comfortable exploring all six.

We chose breadth.

I do not think that was a bad decision.

The final work communicated the opportunity space well.

But I would choose differently if product time, engineering resources, or development cost were involved.

I would prioritize:

1. roadmap;
2. rewards;
3. activity choice;
4. parent visibility.

Then I would test whether those ideas actually improved comprehension, motivation, or continued use before expanding further.

## Where I Would Have Invested More

### Parent experience

I would have developed the parent area further.

Guardians wanted to understand:

- progress;
- changes over time;
- where difficulties appeared;
- how they could support practice;
- what their role should be.

The project had an especially interesting opportunity because one of the founders had a data-science background.

I would want to explore how underlying speech and activity data could be translated into understandable feedback for parents.

Not a clinical dashboard.

A useful one.

Something that could answer:

> What is changing, where is my child struggling, and what can I do next?

### Sensory controls

I would also revisit the visual-control recommendation.

The original design reduced the concept to broad vivid / non-vivid states.

The research suggested much greater individual variation.

I would explore more granular controls and test whether families actually understood and used them.

## What I Would Investigate First Today

If ASD123 hired me tomorrow, I would begin with the **existing product**, not another speculative redesign.

My first research cycle would combine four evidence sources.

### 1. Product analytics

I would first examine what users are already doing.

Questions would include:

- Where do children begin?
- Which activities are selected most frequently?
- Where do sessions end?
- Which activities are repeated?
- How often is the roadmap used?
- Where are users dropping out?
- Do reward interactions correspond with continued play?
- Are clinicians and home users behaving differently?

Analytics would tell me **where to investigate**, not automatically why behavior was happening.

### 2. Heuristic evaluation

I would independently review the current product for:

- navigation;
- feedback;
- consistency;
- accessibility;
- cognitive load;
- clarity of progress;
- error prevention;
- parent/child separation;
- age-appropriate interaction patterns.

That would generate hypotheses before participant testing.

### 3. Observed usability testing with children

This would be the highest-priority primary research.

I would want to observe:

- whether children understand what to do without help;
- where attention shifts;
- whether the roadmap makes sense;
- what rewards actually motivate;
- where sensory or interaction friction appears;
- when adults intervene.

Where ethically and operationally appropriate, I would test the live experience with children in the environments where the product is actually used.

### 4. Interviews with guardians and speech-language pathologists

After observation, I would speak with the adults around the child.

With guardians, I would focus on:

- progress visibility;
- home routines;
- frustration;
- independence;
- parent involvement;
- what they wish the product told them.

With SLPs, I would focus on:

- clinical usefulness;
- activity assignment;
- continuity between sessions;
- evidence of progression;
- how the product fits into therapy rather than competing with it.

The important difference from the original project is that I would now have **behavioral data to compare against what people tell me**.

## Why I Would Sequence It This Way

I would not begin with another large interview study.

The live product already generates evidence.

The strongest research program would move between:

**analytics → expert review → observed behavior → interviews**

Analytics identifies where something interesting or problematic is happening.

Observation shows what users are actually doing.

Interviews help explain context, intent, and needs that behavior alone cannot reveal.

That combination would let me move from designing based primarily on proxy evidence toward a much more complete picture of the child, family, clinician, and product together.
